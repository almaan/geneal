\begin{abstract}
We describe an active-learning framework for nominating gene-knockout targets that
are simultaneously \emph{efficacious} (lethal in a cancer cell line), \emph{safe}
(not broadly essential / not lethal in a reference context), and \emph{mechanistically
diversified} (not concentrated in a single pathway). The method couples Gaussian-process
surrogates over a fixed gene representation, principled acquisition functions
(upper-confidence-bound, determinantal batch selection, and expected hypervolume
improvement for the multi-objective case), a constrained nomination rule that treats
safety as a hard ceiling, and a portfolio hedge that bounds per-pathway exposure to
guard against pathway-level failure. We give the mathematical grounding and rationale
for each step. Data acquisition and representation are out of scope here.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem setup and notation}
Let $\mathcal{G}=\{1,\dots,N\}$ index candidate genes, each with a fixed feature vector
(embedding) $x_i\in\R^d$. For a chosen cell line we define the \emph{efficacy} of
knocking out gene $i$ as the lethality $e_i = -\,\mathrm{effect}_i$, where
$\mathrm{effect}_i$ is the (Chronos) gene-effect score; more negative effect $\Rightarrow$
higher lethality $\Rightarrow$ higher efficacy. We also define a \emph{toxicity}
$t_i\ge 0$ that measures undesired lethality in non-target contexts (\S\ref{sec:tox}).
The goal is to select a batch (portfolio) $B\subset\mathcal{G}$, $|B|=K$, that has high
efficacy and low toxicity while being spread across biological pathways.

Active learning proceeds in rounds. At each round we have a \emph{revealed} set
$D\subset\mathcal{G}$ of genes whose labels (efficacy, and toxicity when unknown) have
been assayed; we fit surrogate models on $D$, score the unrevealed candidates with an
acquisition function, acquire a batch of $q$ new genes, and repeat. All randomness uses
explicit, seeded generators so runs are reproducible, and competing methods share the
same initial set and noise draws (\emph{common random numbers}) for paired comparison.

\section{Gaussian-process surrogate}
We model each scalar objective $y\in\{e,t\}$ as a draw from a Gaussian process,
\begin{equation}
y(x)\sim\mathcal{GP}\big(m(x),\,k(x,x')\big),
\end{equation}
with constant mean $m$ and a Mat\'ern kernel (smoothness $\nu=5/2$) with a scale
factor,
\begin{equation}
k(x,x') = \theta\Big(1+\tfrac{\sqrt{5}r}{\ell}+\tfrac{5r^2}{3\ell^2}\Big)\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{\sqrt5 r}{\ell}\Big),\qquad r=\lVert x-x'\rVert .
\end{equation}
\emph{Why a GP.} Active learning needs \emph{calibrated uncertainty}, not just point
predictions: the acquisition functions below trade off exploiting high predicted values
against exploring uncertain regions. A GP gives a closed-form posterior with both a mean
and a variance. The Mat\'ern-$5/2$ kernel is a standard, mildly smooth default that does
not assume the infinite differentiability of the RBF kernel.

Given revealed inputs $X_D$ and noisy labels $\mathbf{y}_D$ (observation noise
$\sigma_n^2$), the posterior at a test point $x_\ast$ is Gaussian with
\begin{align}
\mu(x_\ast) &= k_\ast^\top (K+\sigma_n^2 I)^{-1}\mathbf{y}_D,\\
\sigma^2(x_\ast) &= k(x_\ast,x_\ast) - k_\ast^\top (K+\sigma_n^2 I)^{-1} k_\ast,
\end{align}
where $K_{ij}=k(x_i,x_j)$ and $(k_\ast)_i=k(x_i,x_\ast)$. For a set of test points we
similarly obtain a joint posterior mean vector and covariance matrix $\Sigma_\ast$.
Kernel hyperparameters $(\theta,\ell,\sigma_n)$ are fit by maximizing the exact log
marginal likelihood
\begin{equation}
\log p(\mathbf{y}_D\mid X_D)= -\tfrac12 \mathbf{y}_D^\top(K+\sigma_n^2 I)^{-1}\mathbf{y}_D
-\tfrac12\log\lvert K+\sigma_n^2 I\rvert -\tfrac{n}{2}\log 2\pi .
\end{equation}
Inputs and targets are standardized before fitting; predictions are returned on the
original scale. For multi-objective problems we fit one independent GP per objective.

\section{Single-objective acquisition}
Given the posterior $(\mu,\sigma)$ over candidates and the best revealed value $y^+$, we
use standard acquisitions:
\begin{align}
\text{UCB:}\quad & a_{\mathrm{UCB}}(x)=\mu(x)+\beta\,\sigma(x),\\
\text{EI:}\quad & a_{\mathrm{EI}}(x)=(\mu(x)-y^+)\,\Phi(z)+\sigma(x)\,\phi(z),\quad z=\tfrac{\mu(x)-y^+}{\sigma(x)},
\end{align}
with $\Phi,\phi$ the standard normal CDF/PDF. \emph{Why.} UCB makes the
explore--exploit trade-off explicit through $\beta$; EI is the expected positive
improvement over the incumbent. Both are maximized (higher score $=$ more desirable).

\section{Diversified batch selection}
When a round acquires $q>1$ genes, taking the top-$q$ by acquisition score can return a
redundant batch. We consider two diversification mechanisms.

\subsection{Quality-weighted $k$-DPP}
A determinantal point process (DPP) over candidates with an $L$-ensemble kernel
$L\in\R^{N\times N}$ assigns a subset $B$ probability
\begin{equation}
\Pr(B)\;\propto\;\det\!\big(L_B\big),
\end{equation}
where $L_B$ is the principal submatrix indexed by $B$. We set
\begin{equation}
L_{ij}=q_i\,S_{ij}\,q_j,\qquad q_i=a(x_i)\ \text{(quality, e.g.\ UCB)},\quad
S_{ij}=\frac{\Sigma_{\ast,ij}}{\sqrt{\Sigma_{\ast,ii}\Sigma_{\ast,jj}}}\ \text{(outcome correlation)} .
\end{equation}
$\det(L_B)$ is large when the chosen genes are both high quality (large diagonal
$q_i^2$) and mutually dissimilar (near-orthogonal rows, small off-diagonal $S$).
\emph{Why this is principled.} For a Gaussian, $\tfrac12\log\det(\text{covariance})$ is
the differential entropy; maximizing $\log\det L_B$ therefore maximizes the joint
information content of the batch. So a quality-weighted $k$-DPP is simultaneously an
information-theoretic and a quality$\times$diversity objective. Exact selection of the
size-$q$ maximizer is NP-hard; we use the greedy MAP rule, which adds at each step the
candidate with the largest marginal gain in $\log\det$ and admits an $O(q^2N)$
incremental Cholesky update.

\subsection{Hard per-pathway cap}
In practice (genome-scale candidate pools) the similarity $S$ is too sparse to create a
tunable trade-off, and a simpler, interpretable hedge is preferred: cap the number of
selected genes per pathway. Given a pathway membership map
$\pi:\mathcal{G}\to 2^{\mathcal{P}}$ (gene $\to$ set of complexes/pathways) and a cap
$c$, we greedily take genes in decreasing quality order, admitting gene $i$ only if no
pathway $p\in\pi(i)$ has already reached $c$ selected members. \emph{Why.} This directly
bounds per-pathway exposure (\S\ref{sec:risk}) with a single, meaningful knob, and is
robust to sparse similarity structure.

\section{Toxicity and the selectivity objective}\label{sec:tox}
We instantiate toxicity in one of three ways:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Common-essential (a priori).} $c_i=\tfrac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbf{1}[\mathrm{effect}_{i,\ell}<\tau_e]$, the fraction of all $L$ cell lines in which knocking out gene $i$ is strongly lethal. Pan-essential genes (high $c_i$) would also kill normal tissue and are the toxicity proxy. This quantity is known prior knowledge from existing screens.
\item \textbf{Contrast line.} $t_i = -\,\mathrm{effect}_{i,B}$, the lethality in a fixed, different-domain reference cell line $B$ (a stand-in for normal tissue). Treated as \emph{unknown} and learned by a second GP.
\item \textbf{Population.} $t_i = -\,\tfrac1L\sum_\ell \mathrm{effect}_{i,\ell}$, mean lethality across the population. Also learned.
\end{enumerate}
A simple scalarized \emph{selective lethality} is $s_i = e_i-\lambda\,t_i$. However, treating
safety as a freely tradeable penalty is unsatisfying (a too-toxic target should be
disqualified, not merely down-weighted), and $\lambda$ has no natural units. We therefore
prefer the constrained formulation below.

\section{Constrained nomination}
We maximize predicted efficacy subject to a hard safety ceiling $\tau$:
\begin{equation}
B^\star=\argmax_{|B|=K}\ \sum_{i\in B}\hat e_i \quad\text{s.t.}\quad \hat t_i\le\tau\ \ \forall i\in B,
\label{eq:constrained}
\end{equation}
which, absent the pathway hedge, reduces to taking the top-$K$ candidates by predicted
efficacy $\hat e_i$ among the \emph{eligible} set $\{i:\hat t_i\le\tau\}$. Sweeping
$\tau$ traces a constrained efficacy--toxicity frontier. \emph{Why a constraint.} It
matches how target nomination actually gates candidates (safety is a bar), gives a single
interpretable knob ($\tau=$ maximum acceptable toxicity), and avoids the scale/unit
ambiguity of $\lambda$. When toxicity is known a priori the constraint uses the known
$c_i$; when learned, $\hat t_i$ is the toxicity-surrogate prediction (optionally an upper
confidence bound for a conservative, high-probability safety guarantee). The per-pathway
cap is layered on \eqref{eq:constrained} to additionally hedge mechanism.

\section{Multi-objective active learning (joint efficacy--toxicity)}
When both efficacy and toxicity are unknown, we explore the two axes jointly. Treat each
gene as a point $f(x)=(e(x),-t(x))$ to be \emph{maximized} in both coordinates.

\paragraph{Dominance and hypervolume.} Point $u$ dominates $v$ if $u\ge v$ componentwise
with strict inequality somewhere. The \emph{Pareto front} of a set is its non-dominated
subset. Given a reference point $r$ (a lower bound in both objectives), the dominated
\emph{hypervolume} is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{HV}(P;r)=\text{area}\Big(\bigcup_{p\in P}[r_1,p_1]\times[r_2,p_2]\Big),
\end{equation}
the area of objective space dominated by $P$ and above $r$. Larger HV $=$ better, broader
coverage of the high-efficacy/low-toxicity region; it is the standard scalar summary of a
multi-objective solution set.

\paragraph{Expected hypervolume improvement (EHVI).} With independent Gaussian posteriors
$f(x)\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol\mu(x),\operatorname{diag}\boldsymbol\sigma^2(x))$ and
current front $P$, we acquire the candidate maximizing
\begin{equation}
a_{\mathrm{EHVI}}(x)=\E_{f(x)}\big[\,\mathrm{HV}(P\cup\{f(x)\};r)-\mathrm{HV}(P;r)\,\big]\;\ge 0 .
\label{eq:ehvi}
\end{equation}
\emph{Why EHVI.} It is the multi-objective analogue of expected improvement: it rewards
candidates expected to most enlarge the dominated region, automatically balancing
exploration (uncertain points can yield large improvement) against exploitation
(promising means) \emph{across the whole frontier}, without choosing a scalarization
weight. We estimate \eqref{eq:ehvi} by Monte Carlo,
\begin{equation}
\hat a_{\mathrm{EHVI}}(x)=\frac1M\sum_{m=1}^{M}\max\!\big(0,\ \mathrm{HV}(P\cup\{f^{(m)}\};r)-\mathrm{HV}(P;r)\big),\quad f^{(m)}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol\mu,\operatorname{diag}\boldsymbol\sigma^2),
\end{equation}
and select a size-$q$ batch greedily: pick the best candidate, add its posterior mean to
the front as a ``fantasy,'' and recompute, $q$ times. To bound cost on large pools we
restrict the Monte-Carlo evaluation to a shortlist of the most promising candidates by an
optimistic score $\mu_1+\sigma_1+\mu_2+\sigma_2$ (a per-objective UCB), which never
excludes a frontier-improving candidate in practice.

\section{Portfolio-risk evaluation}\label{sec:risk}
A nominated portfolio is also judged on \emph{pathway risk}, motivated by the fact that a
pathway may later prove toxic in normal tissue or undruggable, in which case all targets
within it fail together. With pathway membership $\pi$ and (positive) per-gene value
$v_i$ (e.g.\ efficacy), define
\begin{align}
\text{concentration}\quad & C(B)=\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\frac{|\{i\in B:\,p\in\pi(i)\}|}{|\{i\in B:\,\pi(i)\neq\varnothing\}|},\\
\text{dropout robustness}\quad & R(B)=1-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_B|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_B}\frac{\sum_{i\in B:\,p\in\pi(i)} v_i}{\sum_{i\in B} v_i},
\end{align}
where $\mathcal{P}_B$ are the pathways present in $B$. $C(B)$ is the largest share of the
portfolio in any single pathway (lower is safer); $R(B)$ is the expected fraction of
portfolio value that survives if one random present pathway is eliminated (higher is
safer). The per-pathway cap reduces $C$ and raises $R$ at a quantifiable efficacy cost,
producing an efficacy--risk frontier.

\section{Evaluation metrics}
On a held-out objective (e.g.\ true top-$k$ lethal set) we report, as a function of AL
round:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{recall@$k$}: fraction of the true top-$k$ (highest target) genes that have been revealed.
\item \textbf{max value}: best true value among revealed genes, normalized by the global best (the single strongest hit found).
\item \textbf{diversity}: mean pairwise embedding distance of the cumulative revealed set.
\item \textbf{$\alpha$-NDCG@$k$}: a quality-weighted, redundancy-discounted coverage score (Clarke et al.). With nuggets (here, embedding clusters) and graded relevance $g_i$ (lethality), the gain of the gene at rank $i$ covering nugget $n$ is discounted by $(1-\alpha)^{r_{n,i-1}}$, where $r_{n,i-1}$ counts earlier genes already covering $n$; the discounted-cumulative gain is normalized by an ideal greedy ordering. It rewards recovering lethal genes that span \emph{distinct} mechanisms and penalizes redundant ones.
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary of the pipeline}
\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{Risk-aware nomination round (constrained variant)}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State Fit efficacy GP on revealed $(X_D,\mathbf{e}_D)$; if toxicity unknown, fit toxicity GP.
\State Predict $\hat e_i,\hat t_i$ (and uncertainties) for unrevealed candidates.
\State Eligible set $\mathcal{E}=\{i:\hat t_i\le\tau\}$ \Comment{hard safety ceiling}
\State Select $B$: greedily take highest-$\hat e_i$ genes in $\mathcal{E}$, capping $\le c$ per pathway.
\State (AL loop) acquire informative genes by UCB / $k$-DPP / EHVI; reveal labels; repeat.
\State Report efficacy, toxicity, pathway concentration / dropout robustness, recall@$k$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The components compose: GP surrogates provide calibrated predictions and uncertainty;
UCB/$k$-DPP/EHVI choose what to assay; the constrained rule enforces safety as a ceiling;
the per-pathway cap hedges mechanism; and the portfolio-risk and recovery metrics quantify
the efficacy--safety--diversity trade-offs that a target-nomination pipeline must navigate.
